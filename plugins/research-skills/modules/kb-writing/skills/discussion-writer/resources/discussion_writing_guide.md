# Writing the Discussion Section — Guidelines and Template

Summarized from Bem, D.J. (2004). *Writing the Empirical Journal Article*, supplemented with Layer 1 reasoning rules for developmental psychology research.

---

## The Inverse Hourglass Structure

An article follows an hourglass shape. The Discussion is the **bottom half** — it starts specific and progressively broadens:

```
SPECIFIC:  Summary of key findings (what you found, in conceptual terms)
  ↓        Interpretation of findings in light of theory and prior work
  ↓        Comparison with prior literature (convergences, divergences)
  ↓        Alternative explanations and limitations
  ↓        Broader implications (theoretical, practical, methodological)
BROAD:     Future directions and closing statement
```

The Discussion mirrors the Introduction. Topics central to the Introduction reappear here. The sequence of themes is often the inverse of the Introduction's sequence.

## Ten Writing Principles

### 1. Open with What You Learned, Not What You Did

The first sentence should state the implication of your findings, not re-describe the study.

**Wrong:** "In this study, we examined temporal characteristics of neonatal imitation using FPCA." *(Restates what was already in the Introduction and Method.)*

**Right:** "Neonatal tongue protrusion and mouth opening follow distinct temporal trajectories, suggesting they engage different processing mechanisms." *(Leads with the insight.)*

### 2. Each Statement Should Contribute Something New

Do not reformulate and repeat what was said in the Results section. Every sentence should advance the reader's understanding — by interpreting, comparing, or contextualizing.

### 3. Interpret at Multiple Levels

Move from findings close to the data to broader theoretical abstraction. Show how findings connect to larger frameworks.

```
DATA-CLOSE:   "The first FPC captured 68% of variance in tongue protrusion timing..."
  ↓
INTERPRETIVE: "This suggests that the dominant mode of variation is in onset latency..."
  ↓
THEORETICAL:  "...consistent with a perceptual-matching account where visual encoding
               speed drives individual differences in imitative response."
```

### 4. Compare with Prior Work Substantively

Don't just note agreement or disagreement — explain *why* findings converge or diverge.

**Shallow:** "Our finding is consistent with Heimann (1989)." *(Agreement noted, nothing learned.)*

**Substantive:** "The temporal dissociation we observed aligns with Heimann's (1989) finding that tongue protrusion was imitated more reliably than mouth opening. Our data extend this by suggesting the dissociation reflects different processing dynamics, not merely different base rates — tongue protrusion showed earlier peak responses and faster decay."

### 5. Present Competing Explanations

Following the Layer 1 reasoning rule (abductive reasoning with competing explanations):
- Present at least two interpretations for key findings
- Let converging evidence guide which is favored
- Acknowledge remaining uncertainty
- Frame it as evidence narrowing the field, not as a definitive ruling

### 6. Use Cautious Causal Language

Following Layer 1: use "predicted," "associated with," "consistent with," not "caused," "proved," "demonstrated" — unless the design warrants causal claims (e.g., true experiments with random assignment).

### 7. Treat Null Findings as Informative

Following Layer 1 (null findings as informative): Systematically explore four explanations:
1. **Measurement insensitivity** — was the measure too coarse to detect the effect?
2. **Developmental stage** — is the effect not yet present or already consolidated at this age?
3. **Design constraints** — did the procedure limit the opportunity for the effect to emerge?
4. **Genuine absence** — the predicted effect may not exist, and that itself is informative

### 8. Don't Dwell Compulsively on Every Flaw

Acknowledge real limitations that qualify your conclusions, but don't write pretzel-shaped theories to explain away every hiccup in the data. Bem (2004): "Do not end your article with a classic Hollywood disclaimer that names every limitation of the study... The relevance of each limitation depends on the specific findings."

### 9. End with a Bang, Not a Whimper

The closing should be a broad statement of significance, not "further research is needed." Future directions belong earlier in the Discussion — the final sentence should resonate.

**Whimper:** "Future studies should examine these temporal patterns with larger samples." *(Ends on what wasn't done.)*

**Bang:** "The temporal microstructure of neonatal gestures may hold the key to distinguishing genuine intersubjective engagement from reflexive arousal in the first days of life." *(Ends on what this means.)*

### 10. Mind the Correlation Between Length and Clarity

Bem (2004) reports a -.73 correlation between quality of an article and the length of its Discussion section. Say what needs to be said and stop. Cut speculative tangents, consolidate related points, and resist padding.

## Discussion Template: Section-by-Section

### Section 1 — Opening: Summary of Key Findings (1 paragraph)
- Restate the primary finding(s) in conceptual terms — NOT statistical details
- State the implication immediately
- This is NOT a repetition of the Results — it's a statement of what the results *mean*

### Section 2 — Interpretation of Core Findings (2-4 paragraphs, thematic)
- Organize by **theme**, not by finding number
- For each theme: state your interpretation → connect to theoretical framework → compare with prior findings → explain convergences/divergences
- Use argument units (ARG-IDs) as anchors — each theme may correspond to one or more argument units
- Maintain the interpretive ladder: data-close → interpretive → theoretical

### Section 3 — Null or Unexpected Findings (1-2 paragraphs)
- Address non-significant results using the four-explanation framework (measurement, development, design, genuine absence)
- If findings contradict predictions, treat this as an opportunity: what do the unexpected patterns reveal?
- Check competing-explanations for alternative theoretical accounts

### Section 4 — Alternative Explanations and Limitations (1-2 paragraphs)
- Present at least two competing explanations for the key findings
- Use converging evidence to evaluate which is more supported
- Discuss limitations that genuinely qualify your conclusions
- Don't write a laundry list — focus on limitations that change interpretation

### Section 5 — Broader Implications (1-2 paragraphs)
- What does this change about how we understand the phenomenon?
- Theoretical implications (does this support, refine, or challenge existing models?)
- Practical or methodological implications (does this change how we study the topic?)

### Section 6 — Future Directions and Closing (1-2 paragraphs)
- Future directions: specific, actionable research questions (not vague "more research needed")
- Closing statement: broad significance — end with a bang
- This final sentence should echo or advance the broad opening of the Introduction

## Anti-Patterns to Avoid

1. **Results repetition** — Restating findings with statistics rather than interpreting them
2. **Shallow comparison** — "Consistent with X" without explaining *why* or *how*
3. **Single-explanation tunnel vision** — Presenting only your preferred interpretation without alternatives
4. **Limitation laundry list** — Listing every possible flaw without assessing which ones matter
5. **Whimper ending** — Closing with "further research is needed" or a methodological caveat
6. **Citation dump** — String-citing 10 studies without narrating what they collectively show
7. **Pretzel logic** — Tortured explanations for noise in the data
8. **AI filler phrases** — "delve into," "it is important to note," "sheds light on," "nuanced understanding," "underscores the importance"
9. **Throat-clearing openers** — "It is widely acknowledged that..." or "As previously mentioned..."
10. **Speculation without flagging** — Presenting speculative claims as if they were evidence-based

# Writing the Introduction Section — Guidelines and Template

Summarized from Bem, D.J. (2004). *Writing the Empirical Journal Article*. Key principles and template for writing the Introduction section of a psychology research paper.

---

## The Hourglass Structure

An article follows an hourglass shape. The Introduction is the **top half** — it starts broad and progressively narrows to your specific study:

```
BROAD:   General phenomenon or observation in everyday terms
  ↓      Background theory and prior research
  ↓      Specific problem, inconsistencies, or gaps
  ↓      Your study's rationale and purpose
NARROW:  Brief conceptual overview of what you did
```

---

## Four Rules for Opening Statements

1. **Write in English prose, not jargon.** The first sentences should be accessible to any educated reader. Lead from familiar language to technical terms.
2. **Don't plunge readers into the middle.** Take time to lead them from a broad starting point to your specific problem.
3. **Open with something compelling.** A phenomenon, a real-world observation, or a striking finding — not a citation list or a history of the paradigm.
4. **Don't begin with definitions or acronyms.** Define technical terms by context, not by announcement.

### Opening Statement Examples

**Wrong:** "Several years ago, Ekman (1972), Izard (1977), Tomkins (1980), and Zajonc (1980) pointed to psychology's neglect of the affects." *(Citation dump as opener — too specific, no context.)*

**Right:** "Individuals differ radically from one another in the degree to which they are willing and able to express their emotions." *(Broad, accessible, interesting.)*

**Wrong:** "Research in the forced-compliance paradigm has focused on the effects of predecisional alternatives and incentive magnitude." *(Jargon-heavy, assumes reader already knows the paradigm.)*

**Right:** "The corrective lens of hindsight shows cognitive dissonance theory to be one of social psychology's most important contributions." *(Accessible evaluation that leads toward the technical topic.)*

### Introducing Technical Terms

Lead readers from **familiar → unfamiliar** through transition terms:

> "Most people believe... [familiar] → This disharmony among cognitions... [transition] → known as cognitive dissonance [technical term]"

## Literature Review Within the Introduction

### What to Cover
- Summarize the background literature that sets up the problem
- Discuss only research that is **directly relevant** to your study
- Refer readers to existing reviews or meta-analyses for broader coverage
- Emphasize **findings and conclusions**, not procedural details of past studies

### How to Present Prior Research
- Describe a study succinctly by narrating one variation chronologically — this conveys both the overview and procedure simultaneously
- Use **thematic** organization (grouping studies by finding or concept) rather than chronological listing
- Don't let citations dominate the prose — the narrative argument should drive the structure

### Criticizing Previous Work
- Criticize the **work**, not the investigators
- Focus on methodological or theoretical shortcomings that your study addresses

## The "Current Study" Section

End the Introduction with a brief conceptual overview of your own study. This section:
- States the specific purpose and rationale for your study
- Explains how it addresses the gaps identified in the review
- Describes what you did **in conceptual terms** (not procedural details)
- Provides a smooth transition into the Method section

### Example

> "Because this sex difference remains elusive, it seemed desirable to test Zanna's parental-role theory of emotional expression in a more realistic setting. Accordingly, in the study to be presented here, we exposed men and women to filmed scenes designed to evoke either negative or positive emotions and assessed their emotional reactions when they thought they were being observed by one or both of their parents. We also sought to examine the relation of emotional expression to self-esteem."

## Template: Introduction Section Structure

### Section 1 — Opening Hook (1-2 paragraphs)
- Broad, accessible statement about the phenomenon
- Why it matters (real-world significance, prevalence, or stakes)
- Lead naturally to the theoretical domain

### Section 2 — Theoretical Background (2-4 paragraphs)
- Key theoretical framework(s) relevant to the phenomenon
- Define core constructs through context, not announcement
- How the framework explains or predicts relevant outcomes
- Where the framework has been supported or challenged

### Section 3 — Empirical Review (3-6 paragraphs, thematic organization)
- Organize by **theme or finding**, not by individual studies
- For each theme: state the claim → cite converging evidence → note inconsistencies or limitations
- Use recurring concrete examples to maintain thematic coherence
- Highlight where findings converge and where they conflict

### Section 4 — Identifying the Gap (1-2 paragraphs)
- What remains unclear, untested, or contradictory?
- What theoretical prediction lacks empirical evidence?
- What population, context, or mechanism is understudied?
- Why does this gap matter for advancing understanding?

### Section 5 — The Current Study (1-2 paragraphs)
- State the purpose and rationale concisely
- Describe the study in conceptual terms (variables and their relationships, not procedures)
- State research questions or hypotheses
- Preview the contribution — what will this study add?

## Key Anti-Patterns to Avoid

1. **Citation dump opening** — Starting with a list of authors rather than an idea
2. **Chronological lit review** — Organizing past research by date instead of by theme
3. **Procedural descriptions of past studies** — Spending too much space on others' methods
4. **Gap as absence** — "No one has studied X" is not a gap unless you explain why studying X matters
5. **Disconnected sections** — Each paragraph must logically connect to the next; the reader should never wonder "why am I being told this?"
6. **Jargon without build-up** — Introducing technical terms before the reader has context to understand them
7. **Overly broad opening** — "Since the dawn of time..." is too far from the topic to be useful
